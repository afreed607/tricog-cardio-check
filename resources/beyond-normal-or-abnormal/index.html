What Is ECG Risk Stratification? How Tricog CardioCheck Sorts Every ECG into Low, Moderate and High Risk

In short: ECG risk stratification grades an ECG by urgency, not just by diagnosis. Tricog CardioCheck (TCC) sorts every trace into Low, Moderate or High risk, so care teams know exactly how fast to act; reassure and follow up, investigate soon, or escalate immediately; even when no cardiologist is in the room.

What is ECG risk stratification, and why isn’t a diagnosis enough?

A diagnosis names what the heart is doing. Risk stratification answers a different question: how quickly does someone need to respond?

That gap is where care is won or lost. The 2021 AHA/ACC chest pain guideline sets a hard benchmark; an ECG should be obtained and interpreted within 10 minutes of arrival. Acquisition is the easy half. Interpretation, prioritisation and escalation are where queues form, and where a subtle ST-segment change can sit unread behind forty routine traces.

Labelling an ECG “abnormal” doesn’t solve this. Abnormal covers everything from an old inferior infarct in a stable outpatient to an evolving anterior STEMI. Both are abnormal. Only one needs the cath lab activated in the next few minutes. A framework that treats them the same forces every reader to re-derive urgency from scratch, and urgency is exactly the judgement that varies most between a cardiologist and a general physician on a night shift.

How accurate is ECG interpretation without a cardiologist?

Less accurate than most people assume, and the gap widens the further you get from cardiology.

A 2020 systematic review and meta-analysis in JAMA Internal Medicine pooled 78 studies covering 10,056 participants. Without additional ECG training, pooled interpretation accuracy was 42.0% for medical students, 55.8% for residents, 68.5% for practising non-cardiologist physicians and 74.9% for cardiologists and cardiology fellows. Median accuracy across all training levels was 54%, and it rose only to 67% after training interventions.

Two caveats matter, and the authors state both. Accuracy varied enormously between studies; 4% to 95%; so no single number describes real-world performance. And every measurement came from a controlled test setting, which the authors call a best-case scenario; they expect performance in fast-paced clinical practice to be worse.

Read carefully, the finding isn’t that clinicians are careless. It’s that ECG interpretation is genuinely hard, that skill is unevenly distributed, and that a patient’s next step shouldn’t depend on which reader happens to be on shift. That is the case for a shared triage standard.

What do the three TCC risk tiers actually mean?

TCC assigns every ECG to one of three tiers. Each tier maps to an action, not just a label.

What counts as a low-risk ECG?

Low risk covers normal traces and well-recognised benign variants; Normal Sinus Rhythm, Sinus Arrhythmia, Benign Early Repolarisation. These carry no evidence of structural, ischaemic, conduction or rhythm pathology.

The instruction here is routine follow-up, and that matters more than it sounds. Clearing the benign cases confidently is what frees attention for the ones that need it. A triage system that only flags danger, without also giving permission to stand down, still leaves clinicians reviewing everything.

What counts as a moderate-risk ECG?

Moderate risk is real pathology without an active emergency. Old infarctions, suspected ischaemia, complete bundle branch block, atrial enlargement or inter-atrial block, ventricular hypertrophy.

These findings point to underlying cardiovascular disease and need a plan; further evaluation, investigation or monitoring; but not a blue-light response. This tier exists precisely because these cases are the ones that get lost. They are not alarming enough to interrupt a busy shift, and not clean enough to discharge and forget. TCC flags them so they land in a follow-up queue instead of a gap.

What counts as a high-risk ECG?

High risk is built for speed. New-onset or haemodynamically significant atrial fibrillation and flutter, life-threatening arrhythmias, critical conduction disorders, and acute ischaemic patterns; findings carrying real risk of haemodynamic compromise, cardiac arrest or acute ischaemic injury.

The stakes here are well quantified. The American Heart Association notes that atrial fibrillation raises stroke risk roughly fivefold. Detecting AF is only useful if the detection reaches someone who can anticoagulate or rate-control the patient. High-risk classification is what turns a finding into an escalation.

Why does ECG triage matter most outside large cardiac centres?

Globally, an estimated 19.8 million people died from cardiovascular disease in 2022; about 32% of all deaths; and more than three quarters of those deaths occurred in low- and middle-income countries (WHO). That distribution is the whole argument for standardised triage.

The tertiary cardiac centre is not where most cardiac events happen. They happen in district hospitals, standalone diagnostic centres, health camps and Tier-3 clinics, where the ECG machine is present and the cardiologist is not. In those settings, “abnormal” is an unresolved question. “High risk — escalate now” is an instruction anyone on the floor can act on.

A framework that behaves identically in a metro emergency department and a rural clinic also gives a hospital network one triage standard instead of dozens of personal thresholds. That is what makes ECG risk stratification useful at scale, rather than just useful once.

How should a care team use the risk tier in practice?

Treat the tier as the first line of a workflow, not the last word on the patient.

Low risk closes the loop: document, reassure, follow up on schedule. Moderate risk opens one: book the echo, order the troponin, and make sure the patient’s name enters a tracked queue rather than someone’s memory. High risk short-circuits the queue: notify the treating physician immediately, prepare for escalation, and stabilise while confirmation is underway. Everyone in the chain reads the same signal and takes the same next step.

Does risk stratification replace the treating physician?

No, and it shouldn’t be sold as though it does.

TCC risk stratification is a triage aid. Every tier is meant to be read alongside the patient’s clinical presentation and confirmed through proper diagnostic evaluation with a 12-lead ECG. An asymptomatic patient with a moderate-risk trace and a patient with crushing chest pain and the same trace are not the same case, and no tiering system can tell them apart on its own.

What the framework removes is ambiguity in the first step; knowing how serious an ECG is the moment it’s recorded. In cardiac care, that step is measured in minutes, and minutes are the resource nobody gets back.

FAQ

Is ECG risk stratification the same as an ECG diagnosis?

No. A diagnosis identifies the abnormality; risk stratification grades how urgently it needs attention. The same diagnosis can carry different urgency depending on whether it’s new, acute or haemodynamically significant.

Can a moderate-risk ECG still be serious?

Yes. Moderate risk means real cardiovascular pathology without an active emergency. It requires a follow-up plan; further evaluation, investigation or monitoring; not dismissal.

Who is ECG risk stratification most useful for?

Referring physicians, technicians and care teams working without an on-site cardiologist, and hospital networks that want one consistent triage standard across every location.
