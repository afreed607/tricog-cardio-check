What Is ECG Risk Stratification? How AI Can Help Sort ECGs by Risk

An ECG can tell you a lot about the heart. But there is another question that matters just as much: how urgently does this ECG need attention?

That is where ECG risk stratification comes in.

Tricog CardioCheck (TCC) uses a single-lead ECG to classify cardiovascular risk into Low, Moderate or High, helping healthcare teams decide which patients can continue through routine care and which may need further evaluation.

What is ECG risk stratification?

An ECG diagnosis tells you what was detected.

Risk stratification asks a different question:

“How urgently does this finding need attention?”

That distinction matters.

In a busy healthcare setting, getting the ECG is only part of the process.

Someone still needs to interpret it, decide whether the finding matters and determine what happens next.

That becomes harder when one clinician is looking at dozens of ECGs, or when a cardiologist is not immediately available.

The 2021 AHA/ACC chest pain guideline recommends that an ECG be obtained and interpreted within 10 minutes for patients presenting with acute chest pain. The point is simple: when cardiac risk may be time-sensitive, an ECG needs to lead to action quickly.

Calling an ECG “abnormal” does not tell you what to do next.

An ECG can show an old, stable finding. It can also show a pattern that needs urgent attention.

Both may be labelled “abnormal”.

The urgency is very different. The challenge is not simply finding an abnormal ECG. It is deciding which abnormal ECG needs attention first.

How accurate is ECG interpretation without a cardiologist?

ECG interpretation is a specialised skill, and accuracy can vary depending on the reader's training and experience.

A 2020 systematic review published in JAMA Internal Medicine looked at 78 studies involving more than 10,000 participants. ECG interpretation accuracy varied across different levels of medical training, from medical students to cardiologists and cardiology fellows.

The review reported pooled accuracy of 42.0% for medical students, 55.8% for residents, 68.5% for practising non-cardiologist physicians and 74.9% for cardiologists and cardiology fellows.

The important point is not that non-cardiologists cannot read ECGs. It is that ECG interpretation is difficult, and performance varies considerably across readers and clinical settings.

There is an important caveat: accuracy varied widely between the studies, from 4% to 95%. So these numbers should not be treated as a single measure of how accurately every clinician reads an ECG in everyday practice.

And this does not mean clinicians are careless. It’s that ECG interpretation is genuinely hard, that skill is unevenly distributed, and that a patient’s next step shouldn’t depend on which reader happens to be on shift. That is where a consistent ECG triage system can help.

What do the three TCC risk tiers actually mean?

TCC groups ECG results into three risk levels: Low, Moderate and High. The idea is simple: the result should help the care team understand what needs attention next.

What counts as a low-risk ECG?

A Low-risk ECG may include a normal ECG or recognised benign patterns such as Normal Sinus Rhythm, Sinus Arrhythmia or Benign Early Repolarisation.

In these cases, there may be no ECG finding that requires urgent escalation. The patient can continue through the appropriate routine care pathway based on their clinical situation.

Low risk does not mean “ignore it.” It means there is no indication from the ECG alone for urgent escalation. Clearing the benign cases confidently is what frees attention for the ones that need it. A triage system that only flags danger, without also giving permission to stand down, still leaves clinicians reviewing everything.

What counts as a moderate-risk ECG?

Moderate risk is where things get interesting. Depending on the classification criteria, examples may include previous infarction patterns, suspected ischaemia, bundle branch block, atrial enlargement or ventricular hypertrophy.

These findings may need further evaluation, investigation or monitoring. The key is making sure the patient does not disappear into the “we’ll look at it later” pile. This tier exists precisely because these cases are the ones that get lost. They are not alarming enough to interrupt a busy shift, and not clean enough to discharge and forget. TCC flags them so they land in a follow-up queue instead of a gap.

What counts as a high-risk ECG?

High risk is built for speed.

New-onset or haemodynamically significant atrial fibrillation and flutter, life-threatening arrhythmias, critical conduction disorders, and acute ischaemic patterns; findings carrying real risk of haemodynamic compromise, cardiac arrest or acute ischaemic injury.

Atrial fibrillation is a good example of why detection alone is not enough. AF is associated with a substantially higher risk of stroke, which means identifying it needs to be connected to appropriate clinical evaluation and management. Finding a risk signal is only useful if someone knows what to do with it. High-risk classification is what turns a finding into an escalation.

Why is ECG risk stratification useful in clinics and smaller hospitals?

A cardiologist cannot be everywhere.

That is particularly relevant in primary care clinics, diagnostic centres, community screening programs and smaller hospitals where an ECG may be available even when specialist cardiac expertise is not immediately on site.

The question then becomes: how can a care team identify which ECGs need more attention?

The tertiary cardiac centre is not where most cardiac events happen. They happen in district hospitals, standalone diagnostic centres, health camps and Tier-3 clinics, where the ECG machine is present and the cardiologist is not. In those settings, “abnormal” can leave the next step unclear.

A risk category can make the starting point clearer: Low — routine care. Moderate — investigate. High — escalate.

For healthcare networks, a standardised approach can also make ECG triage more consistent across different locations and teams. That is what makes ECG risk stratification useful at scale, rather than just useful once.

How should a care team use the risk tier in practice?

Think of the risk tier as the start of the workflow, not the final diagnosis.

  • Low risk: continue with the appropriate routine care pathway.
  • Moderate risk: review the finding and determine whether further evaluation, investigation or monitoring is needed.
  • High risk: prompt immediate clinical review and escalation according to the patient’s presentation and local protocol.

ECG risk stratification: the key points

✓

✓

✓

Does risk stratification replace the treating physician?

No. ECG risk stratification is a clinical support tool, not a replacement for the treating physician.

TCC is designed to support triage and risk assessment from a single-lead ECG. The result should be considered alongside the patient's symptoms, history, examination and other clinical information. A risk category is not the same thing as a final diagnosis. Patients who require further assessment should be evaluated through the appropriate clinical pathway, including confirmatory testing where indicated.

What risk stratification can provide is a clearer starting point. Instead of asking only, “Is this ECG abnormal?”, the care team can also ask, “What level of attention does this result require?”

FAQ

Is ECG risk stratification the same as an ECG diagnosis?

No. ECG diagnosis identifies or describes an ECG finding. ECG risk stratification groups the result according to its level of clinical concern or urgency. They serve different purposes.

Can a moderate-risk ECG still be serious?

Yes. Moderate risk does not mean that an ECG finding is unimportant. It generally indicates that further clinical evaluation or follow-up may be appropriate rather than immediate emergency escalation.

Who is ECG risk stratification most useful for?

ECG risk stratification can be useful for healthcare teams in primary care, clinics, diagnostic centres, community screening programs and hospitals where ECGs are performed as part of routine care. It can be particularly useful where access to specialist ECG interpretation is limited or delayed.
